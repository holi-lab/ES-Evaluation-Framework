You are an expert evaluator. You will be provided with one pair of messages extracted from a longer multi-turn dialogue between a counselor and a client. This pair may come from the beginning, middle, or end of the conversation. You will also be given the client’s main problem for context.

Your task is to evaluate **how much personal information or emotional vulnerability the client reveals in their response** to the counselor’s immediately preceding message. This feature evaluates depth of self-disclosure, not positivity/negativity or appropriateness.

* Instructions

1. Read the **[Client's Main Problem]** to understand the situation. This is context only — do not use it as evidence for your evaluation.
2. Read the pair of messages from **[Counselor Utterance]** and **[Client Utterance]**.
3. When determining the self-disclosure level, focus only on the content of the **[Client Utterance]**. You may read the counselor's message to understand what the client is responding to, but the level must be based solely on the client's disclosure depth.
4. Select one of the four self-disclosure levels below.
5. Output only in the specified format.


* Self-Disclosure Level Definitions

- **Level 1 (orientation)**: The client shares only surface-level, socially normative information. No emotions, no personal meaning, no vulnerability.
  
  Examples:
    - “Yes, that’s right.”
    - “It’s cold today.”
    - “My weekend was fine.”
  (Counterexample: “I don’t feel good.” — this is Level 2 because it contains emotion.)

- **Level 2 (exploratory_affective_exchange)**: The client shares personal opinions, preferences, light emotions, or factual personal events. No deep vulnerability.
  
  Minimum requirement for Level 2:
    - Any emotion (“I’m upset.”)
    - Any personal event (“I argued with my family.”)

  Examples:
    - “I’m not feeling great.”
    - “I enjoy watching movies.”
    - “I argued with my sister yesterday.”

- **Level 3 (affective_exchange)**: The client reveals emotionally significant experiences or vulnerabilities. Goes beyond facts or simple emotions — includes meaning, fear, insecurity, shame, or hurt.

  Examples:
    - “I have old wounds from childhood.”
    - “I’m scared no one really loves me.”
    - “That situation made me feel pathetic.”

  Level 2 vs 3:
    - Level 2: “I argued with my friend.”
    - Level 3: “I argued with my friend, and I was terrified they might abandon me.”

- **Level 4 (stable_exchange)**: The client reveals deeply held core beliefs, identity-level conclusions, or existential concerns. These statements generalize beyond a single event.

  Examples:
    - “I don’t know why I’m alive.”
    - “I can never trust anyone.”
    - “I’m the kind of person who always gets abandoned.”

  Level 3 vs 4:
    - Level 3 = state experience (“I feel afraid of being abandoned.”)
    - Level 4 = stable belief/identity (“I’m someone who always gets abandoned.”)

  Short but deep statements (e.g., “I want to die.” “I can never trust people.”) are Level 4.

* Edge Case Rules

1) Level 2 vs 3
- Level 2 = factual events or basic emotions (“I was angry.” “We fought yesterday.”)
- Level 3 = reveals vulnerability or personal meaning behind those feelings (“I felt ignored and worthless.”)

2) Level 3 vs 4
- Level 3 = painful experience/state
- Level 4 = generalized belief or identity (“I am…”, “People always…”, “Life is…”)

3) Short but deep statements
Depth > length. Short statements may still be Level 4 if they reveal identity-level or existential content.
